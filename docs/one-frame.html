System 16B → Sega 32X · how the port draws a frame

One Frame of Altered Beast

A 1988 arcade board draws its picture with four dedicated chips, for free, every scanline. A 1994 Sega 32X has two fast CPUs and a framebuffer where every pixel is a store instruction. This page follows a sprite sheet from the ROM dumps on disk through both machines to the screen, and shows why the seven-year-younger console can keep up only by asking the Mega Drive's own video chip to help.

01Two machines, one picture

System 16B is a 68000 at 10 MHz surrounded by video silicon. The CPU never touches a pixel: it writes tile numbers into tile RAM, sprite records into sprite RAM, colours into palette RAM, and the chips read those tables and paint the beam. The Mega Drive is built the same way, only smaller: its VDP draws two tile planes and 80 sprites from 64 KB of VRAM with 64 colours. The 32X adds two SH-2s, 256 KB of SDRAM and a 320×224 framebuffer whose output is mixed with the Mega Drive's video, pixel by pixel, on the way to the TV.

02From ROM dumps to a cartridge image

Nothing is redrawn by hand. The build takes the arcade ROM set the author owns and converts each chip's contents into the shape the target CPU wants, at build time, once. The arcade's own program runs unmodified except for address patches. MAME's -listxml manifest tells the tools how the ROM files interleave, so the same scripts serve any System 16B title.

03Rewiring the game's addresses

The arcade program writes to hardware at fixed addresses: tile RAM at 0x400000, sprite RAM at 0x440000, palette at 0x840000. On the Mega Drive those addresses are empty. patch_game.py disassembles the binary, finds every absolute operand in a hardware range and rewrites it to a place the Mega Drive 68000 can reach. Two destinations exist: Mega Drive work RAM, which the 68000 owns outright, and the 32X framebuffer window, which the 68000 sees at 0x840000 and the SH-2s see at 0x24000000. Whatever the game writes there, the SH-2s can read in place.

04What a sprite is

A System 16B sprite is not a bitmap. It is a 16-byte record that tells the sprite chip where to start reading ROM, how far to step per row, how much to shrink, and which of 64 colour sets to paint with. The chip walks the ROM a word at a time, four pixels per word, until it meets a nibble of 0xF, which ends the row. Rows can be any width, so a frame is a ragged strip, not a rectangle. Zoom is an accumulator that skips source pixels as it overflows, in 32 steps down to half size. The werewolf transformation rides on this, and it is the first reason sprites cannot go to the Mega Drive's sprite hardware.

05What a tile is, and who is in front of whom

The two scroll planes and the text layer are grids of 8×8 tiles. Each cell in tile RAM is one 16-bit word naming a tile and one of 128 colour sets of eight pens. Bit 15 marks a priority tile, one that draws in front of most sprites. The tile pixels themselves are three bitplanes in three separate ROM chips, so a pen is 0 to 7. The arcade mixer then sorts ten layers per pixel: two text categories, two foreground categories, two background categories, and sprites at four priority levels interleaved between them. That interleaving is the second, subtler, reason the split across two chips is hard.

06The 68000's frame

From here on, the page follows one frame at 60 Hz. NTSC gives 262 scanlines per frame, and the arcade game was written for a 10 MHz 68000. On the Mega Drive it runs at 7.67 MHz, and it shares its frame with the shim: the vertical-interrupt handler that delivers the game's tables to the SH-2s and the VDP. The game's own frame protocol is unforgiving. It clears a flag, runs its pass, and spins until its interrupt sets the flag again, so a frame is exactly one vint or exactly two. Nothing in between.

07The handoff: one packet per vint

Everything the SH-2s need from the game that does not already sit in the framebuffer crosses in one packet, once per vint, through the 32X's DREQ FIFO. The 68000 writes words into a register; the SH-2 master's DMA controller drains them into SDRAM with no CPU involved. The protocol around it exists because the FIFO drops words when the SH-2 is busy on the bus, so the packet carries an exact length and a magic tail, and a torn landing is reported back so the 68000 can re-send what was lost.

08The SH-2s draw a generation

The SH-2 side works in generations, and a generation is a whole frame. Nothing smaller is ever scheduled or shipped, because an earlier design that composed and shipped the screen in three bands let one band freeze while the others moved, which the eye reads instantly. The master latches every input of the frame at one instant, hands the slave one command, and the slave composes the entire screen into a staging buffer in SDRAM. Only when the slave has echoed completion does the frame ship, both CPUs blitting half each, inside the window where they own the framebuffer. The vertical-interrupt handler then flips only a bank that was freshly written.

09Where everything lives

Two memory maps carry the whole pipeline. The 256 KB of 32X SDRAM holds the SH-2 program, the hot code copied out of ROM, every shadow of the game's tables, the staging buffer and the DMA landing zone. Each 128 KB framebuffer bank holds the 320×224 image and, past it, the game's own tile RAM: the 68000 writes there because the patcher told it to, and the SH-2s read it in place.

10The Mega Drive draws the planes

The VDP cannot read SH-2 SDRAM and the 68000 cannot read the arcade tile ROM in the SH-2's format, so the scroll planes reach the VDP through a second packet that travels the other way: SH-2 to 68000, through the dead 1,536 bytes past the framebuffer image. Inside its FM window the master converts a batch of arcade tiles into the VDP's 4-bits-per-pixel planar format, remapping each tile's eight pens into the slot its colour set was given in one of three MD palette lines. At the top of the next vint, with FM low, the 68000 reads the packet through its own window and DMAs it into VRAM, the scroll table and CRAM.

11The composite: one pixel, two chips

At the very end, the 32X cartridge mixes its framebuffer with the Mega Drive's video output before the signal leaves the console. Each framebuffer byte indexes the 32X colour RAM; if the entry it lands on has bit 15 set, the mixer shows the Mega Drive's pixel at that position instead. The staging buffer is cleared to index 0, whose entry is the one through-colour, so everywhere the SH-2s drew nothing the VDP's planes show. Everywhere they drew a sprite, a priority tile or a glyph, the 32X colour wins.

12Why it works, and what is left

The 32X keeps up with the arcade board for the same reason the arcade board was fast: most of the screen is drawn by silicon that was built to draw it. The Mega Drive's VDP scrolls two full planes for one register write. The SH-2s spend their bandwidth only on what the VDP cannot express, and the framebuffer they fill is mostly holes. What the ladder says. The 68000, not the SH-2s, is the binding constraint now. Its frame must lose about 50 lines to cross the 262-line threshold, and the two largest non-game items in its vint are the palette compare (16 lines) and the DMA of the Mega Drive's plane data (11 lines). Neither moves the needle alone; together, plus a proven row-skip on the blit, they do. The next lever named in the handoff is moving the palette compare onto the SH-2.

What is still owned by the arcade's shape

  • Zoomed sprites stay on the SH-2s forever. No 32X or Mega Drive silicon scales a sprite, and Altered Beast zooms constantly.
  • Priority sprites at levels 0 and 1 draw in front of the VDP planes. The layer order stands in for the arcade's per-pixel gate; it is the one place the split is not exact.
  • Sound is a separate port. The arcade's YM2151 has eight FM channels; the Mega Drive's YM2612 has six, so a faithful score means a software synth on an SH-2 driving the 32X PWM. Designed, not built.
  • Everything else generalises. Every System 16B title has two scroll planes, a text layer, this sprite chip and 128 colour sets. The patcher, the packet, the residency allocator and the palette pack are the kit.

What does our machine actually do?Two targets. ares, the cycle-accurate 32X emulator, forked to add a headless mode: it runs scripted input files with no window, dumps savestates and screenshots, and tools/state_health.py and tools/gameplay_speed.py read the pipeline counters straight out of SDRAM. Every number on this page comes from those runs. FPGA 32X emulation (the MiSTer core) and the author's play pass are the hardware-truth check and the acceptance gate.

MAME's 32X model is kept for what it does honestly, the 68000 and VDP timing, and not trusted for framebuffer cost, which it does not model at all: the same blit measures 9.4 µs per row there and 47.3 on ares. It also lands none of the DREQ packets a current build sends, so it cannot judge pixels on this line. That is why the accurate emulator had to become scriptable: the slow target is the only one whose answer counts, and a play pass cannot be re-run a thousand times.
